# Designing the narrative

A narrative selects facts and gives them meaning, so set the point of view and the central change before you start writing. Prefer the reader accurately understanding the relationship among the situation, the judgment, and the future, over making the piece look like a tidy story.

## Design procedure

### 1. Set the reader and the purpose

- Who reads this. What should they come to know, and what should they be able to judge?
- What you want to share is one of: a reading of the situation, a strategy, the need for a change, or a set of options.
- Are you asking for a decision or an action, or building a base for understanding and discussion?

### 2. Separate evidence from interpretation

Sort the material into at least these buckets.

- Confirmed facts and data
- Views that sources or the people involved state
- This report's interpretation and inference
- Predictions, goals, and wishes
- Unchecked premises and uncertainty

Do not present an interpretation as if it were a fact, or a goal as if it were a future already fixed. When the evidence you need is missing, do not strengthen the narrative. Make the items to confirm, and the reservations, explicit.

### 3. Choose the central change

Put one change or tension that the reader needs to understand at the center. When there are several themes, rank them. Do not bundle everything into one large transformation story.

The shape of the central change differs by field. For example:

- The experimental result improved, but in the environment where it is used a different constraint governs performance.
- The premise of a policy changed, and a support measure that used to work now reaches people differently.
- Market expectations changed, and the gap with the value that used to be offered is widening.

### 4. Draft the narrative's thread in one sentence

The following shape is a working device for organizing the idea. It is not a template to drop into the body as-is.

> **<Situation>** has produced **<a change or tension>**. Because this has **<meaning>**, **<the choices and roles of the people involved>** need to be considered toward **<the state to aim for>**.

If you cannot say it in one sentence, check whether the central change, the reader, and the purpose are fixed before you add information.

### 5. Unfold it in an order that fits the reader

You are not limited to telling it as a timeline from the situation to the future. For example, use flows such as these.

- **Start from the change:** what changed → why it matters → what to choose
- **Start from the judgment:** question and options → axes of judgment → evidence → effects and the next step
- **Start from the future:** the state to aim for → the gap with the present → the path → roles of the people involved
- **Start from a reading of the present:** the present → where it diverges from the existing view → a new interpretation → implications

Do not turn this flow into a fixed table of contents. Reorder or drop parts to fit the question, the evidence, and how the reader reads.

### 6. Make the roles of the people involved concrete

Do not depict the reader or the people involved only as an audience to be persuaded. Distinguish who has the decision, who has responsibility for carrying it out, and who is affected. Do not assume that everyone shares the same values or interests. Show the choices they can take part in, and the real constraints.
